# Source-audited additions — Module 10

## m10-u01

**InnerSource** applies proven open-source collaboration practices inside an organization while keeping source code within the organization's chosen visibility and governance boundary. The objective is not to make proprietary code public; it is to reduce organizational silos by making internal projects easier for other teams to discover, understand, reuse, and contribute to under explicit rules.

Successful InnerSource therefore combines technology and operating model. Repositories need clear ownership and contribution guidance, discoverable documentation, issues/PRs for transparent work, review rules, and maintainers who are prepared to accept contributions from outside their immediate team. Organizations also need to decide which repositories are suitable for broader internal reuse, what information must remain restricted, and how permissions/identity/compliance policies apply.

The open-source analogy is useful but not absolute: the contributors are usually employees or trusted internal collaborators, organization policy still governs access, and an internal repository may use **internal/private visibility** rather than a public open-source license. The key behavioral shift is from "only our team can change this" to "our team stewards this project and other authorized teams can propose improvements through a documented process."

## m10-u05

A workable InnerSource program has several recurring elements: identify projects that other teams can reuse; make them discoverable; document purpose/setup/API/architecture; define CONTRIBUTING expectations; expose ownership through CODEOWNERS or equivalent maintainer information; use Issues/Discussions for visible planning; and use pull requests/reviews as the normal contribution boundary.

Governance should scale with risk. A reusable internal library might allow broad read access but require code-owner approval for sensitive areas. An organization can use templates, repository standards, automation, rulesets, and metrics to make contribution consistent without requiring every team to invent its own process.

The health of an InnerSource program is not measured only by how many repositories are marked internal. Useful signals include cross-team contributors, reused components, accepted external-to-team pull requests, review responsiveness, documentation quality, and whether teams can find the correct maintainers. The goal is sustainable internal collaboration and reuse, not simply opening permissions.
